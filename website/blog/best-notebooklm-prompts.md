## How to use these prompts effectively

- **Name the format** — "bullet list," "table," or "flashcard pairs" beats open-ended asks.
- **Name the audience** — "for a first-year biology student" sharpens tone and depth.
- **Follow up** — treat the first answer as a draft; ask for deeper dives on weak sections.
- **Scope to sources** — add "based only on my uploaded sources" when you want strict grounding.

1. ## 1. Executive summary
  Prompt
  Summarize all sources in this notebook in 8-10 bullet points. Lead with the main thesis or conclusion, then supporting evidence. Flag any contradictions between sources.
  **When to use:** First pass on a new notebook — orient yourself before deep reading. Works well after importing a lecture series or reading packet.
2. ## 2. Key terms glossary
  Prompt
  Create a glossary of the 20 most important terms across my sources. For each term: a one-sentence definition, which source introduced it, and one example.
  **When to use:** Technical courses, law, medicine, or any field with dense vocabulary. Export the result to your own notes for quick review.
3. ## 3. Compare and contrast
  Prompt
  Compare how each source treats [TOPIC]. Present as a table with columns: Source, Main claim, Evidence used, Limitations.
  **When to use:** Literature reviews, debate prep, or seminar papers where authors disagree. Replace [TOPIC] with your specific question.
4. ## 4. Study outline
  Prompt
  Build a hierarchical study outline from my sources. Use H2 for major topics, H3 for subtopics, and bullet points for facts I must memorize. Order by logical dependency, not source order.
  **When to use:** Exam season — turns scattered readings into a single study map. Pair with our [NotebookLM study guide](https://www.notebooktools.com/blog/how-to-use-notebooklm-to-study) for workflow tips.
5. ## 5. Practice exam questions
  Prompt
  Generate 15 practice exam questions based only on my sources. Mix multiple choice (with explanations), short answer, and one essay question. Include answer keys at the end.
  **When to use:** Active recall beats passive re-reading. Run this a week before the exam, then ask follow-ups on questions you miss.
6. ## 6. Flashcard generator
  Prompt
  Create 30 flashcard pairs (Question:: Answer) from my sources. Focus on definitions, cause-effect relationships, and formulas. Format each pair on its own line.
  **When to use:** Memorization-heavy subjects. Copy into Anki or your preferred flashcard app.
7. ## 7. Methodology extractor
  Prompt
  For each research paper in this notebook, extract: research question, methodology, sample size (if stated), key findings, and stated limitations. Present as a structured table.
  **When to use:** Graduate seminars, thesis lit reviews, or any notebook heavy on academic PDFs.
8. ## 8. Argument map
  Prompt
  Map the logical structure of the main argument across my sources. Show: central claim → supporting premises → counterarguments addressed → conclusion. Note gaps where sources do not provide evidence.
  **When to use:** Philosophy, policy analysis, or persuasive writing courses where structure matters.
9. ## 9. Timeline builder
  Prompt
  Extract every date, event, and sequence mentioned across my sources. Build a chronological timeline from earliest to latest. Flag uncertain dates.
  **When to use:** History, political science, or case studies with evolving facts over time.
10. ## 10. ELI5 explainer
  Prompt
  Explain the core concepts in this notebook as if teaching a smart high school student. Use analogies, avoid jargon unless you define it, and end with three 'check your understanding' questions.
  **When to use:** When a textbook chapter or lecture video left you confused. Especially useful after [adding YouTube lectures](https://www.notebooktools.com/blog/youtube-to-notebooklm) to your notebook.
11. ## 11. Source gap analysis
  Prompt
  Based on my current sources, what important questions about [TOPIC] remain unanswered? List 5 gaps and suggest what type of source (paper, expert interview, dataset) would fill each gap.
  **When to use:** Early research planning — before you waste time reading the wrong materials.
12. ## 12. Briefing memo
  Prompt
  Write a one-page briefing memo on [TOPIC] for a busy decision-maker. Sections: Situation, Key findings (with source references), Risks, Recommended next steps. Max 400 words.
  **When to use:** Workplace research, consulting, or capstone projects where you need a polished deliverable — not just study notes.

## NotebookLM study prompts by subject

The prompts above work for any course. These versions are tuned for subjects where students search for NotebookLM prompts most. Add your lecture slides, textbook chapters and past papers first, then paste a prompt into chat.

### Nursing students

Prompt

Using only my sources, write 10 NCLEX-style questions on [TOPIC]. Mix select-all-that-apply and prioritization questions. After each, give the correct answer, a rationale, and why each wrong option is wrong, with a citation to the source.

Build a drug card table from my sources for every medication mentioned: class, mechanism, indications, key side effects, contraindications and nursing considerations. Mark any field the sources do not cover as "not in sources".

### Medical students

Create a clinical vignette for [CONDITION] based only on my sources, then ask me for the most likely diagnosis, the next best investigation and first-line management. Reveal the answers with citations only after I reply.

### Law students

For each case in my sources, give: facts in two sentences, the legal issue, the holding, the reasoning and how it relates to the other cases. Then write one hypothetical exam problem that tests the distinctions between them.

### Scholars and PhD researchers

Compare how the papers in this notebook define and measure [CONCEPT]. Present a table of definitions, methods and findings, then list where the papers contradict each other and which open questions a new study could address. Cite every row.

### Any exam: the weekly review prompt

List the 10 concepts my lecturer stresses most across these sources, ranked by how often they appear. For each, give a one-line definition and one question I should be able to answer before the exam.

## Prompt patterns that always help

Beyond specific wording, three patterns improve almost every NotebookLM session:

- **Constrain length** — "in 5 bullets" or "max 300 words" prevents rambling.
- **Request citations** — "cite which source supports each claim" keeps answers honest.
- **Iterate** — "go deeper on point 3" or "rewrite for a non-expert audience" refines the first draft.

If prompts return thin answers, the problem is usually thin sources — not the prompt itself. Add another paper, import a complementary video, or paste a relevant article. ## FAQ

### What are the best prompts to use in NotebookLM?

Start with structured requests: executive summaries, key-term glossaries, compare-and-contrast across sources, and exam-style practice questions. Specific prompts that name the output format (bullet list, table, flashcards) produce better results than vague questions.

### How do I get better answers from NotebookLM?

Add more high-quality sources, ask follow-up questions, and specify audience and format. Import videos and articles with NotebookTools so your notebook has richer material to ground answers.

### Can NotebookLM prompts work on YouTube sources?

Yes. After adding a YouTube video as a source, use prompts like ‘Summarize this lecture in 10 bullet points’ or ‘List every concept I need for the midterm.’ See our guide on [summarizing YouTube videos in NotebookLM](https://www.notebooktools.com/blog/youtube-to-notebooklm) for the full workflow.

### What are good NotebookLM prompts for nursing students?

Ask NotebookLM for NCLEX-style questions with rationales built only from your sources, and a drug card table covering class, mechanism, side effects and nursing considerations. Both prompts are in the study prompts by subject section.

### Can I save prompts in NotebookLM?

NotebookLM does not have a built-in prompt library. Bookmark our [prompt library](https://www.notebooktools.com/blog/category/prompts), keep a personal snippet doc, or save prompts in the NotebookTools extension and insert them by typing / in chat.

### Do prompts work in Audio Overviews?

Not directly. Audio Overviews are generated from the sources in your notebook. You can usually add a short instruction about focus when you create one, but the prompts on this page are written for the chat.
